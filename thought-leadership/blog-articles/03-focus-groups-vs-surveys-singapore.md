## The Question We Get Asked Most

"Should we do focus groups or surveys?"

After 600+ projects in Singapore, this is still the most common question clients ask. And the honest answer is: it depends. Not on budget alone, but on what you need to learn, who you need to learn it from, and what decisions the research needs to support.

Here is a practical decision framework based on what actually works in the Singapore market.

---

## When Focus Groups Are the Right Choice

### You need to understand the "why"
Surveys can tell you that 62% of Singaporean women aged 25-35 prefer Brand A over Brand B. Focus groups tell you *why* — and the reasons are often cultural, emotional, or contextual in ways that survey data alone cannot capture.

### You're exploring a new category or concept
If you don't yet know the right questions to ask, you're not ready for a survey. Focus groups are exploratory by nature. They surface language, concerns, and decision factors you didn't anticipate — which then inform better survey design.

### Cultural nuance matters
In Singapore's multicultural context, the same product can mean different things to different ethnic groups. Focus groups — especially when moderated bilingually — capture these nuances in ways that standardised survey questions cannot.

### Your audience is niche or hard-to-reach
For specialist segments (HCPs, C-suite executives, high-net-worth individuals), focus groups or in-depth interviews yield richer data from smaller sample sizes. A 90-minute conversation with 8 healthcare professionals gives you more actionable insight than 200 checkbox responses.

### You need to observe group dynamics
Purchase decisions in Singapore are often influenced by family, peer groups, and social proof. Focus groups let you observe how opinions form, shift, and solidify in a social context — which mirrors real-world decision-making.

**Typical focus group project in Singapore:**
- 4-6 groups of 6-8 participants each
- 90 minutes per session
- Bilingual moderation (English + Mandarin or Malay)
- Timeline: 4-6 weeks (recruitment through reporting)
- Starting from: SGD 3,000 per group

## When Surveys Are the Right Choice

### You need to quantify
If the decision-maker needs numbers — market sizing, preference shares, NPS scores, purchase intent — a survey is the right tool. Surveys give you statistical confidence that focus groups, by design, cannot.

### You're validating a hypothesis
Once you've used qualitative research to understand the landscape, surveys let you test specific hypotheses at scale. "Do 60%+ of our target segment prefer Feature A over Feature B?" is a survey question.

### You need to segment your market
Surveys with sufficient sample size (n=400+) allow you to segment consumers by demographics, psychographics, or behaviour patterns. This is critical for targeting and positioning decisions.

### You need a benchmark
If you plan to track metrics over time (brand health, customer satisfaction, NPS), surveys provide the repeatable, comparable data that tracking studies require.

### Your budget or timeline is constrained
Online surveys in Singapore are generally faster and more cost-effective than focus groups, especially for straightforward measurement objectives.

**Typical survey project in Singapore:**
- Sample size: n=400-1,000
- 10-15 minute questionnaire
- Online fieldwork via proprietary panel
- Timeline: 3-5 weeks (design through reporting)
- Starting from: SGD 8,000

## When You Need Both (And Why)

For most significant business decisions in Singapore, the answer is: you need both. Here's why.

**The sequential approach works best:**

1. **Start with focus groups** (exploratory) to understand the landscape, generate hypotheses, and identify the right questions.
2. **Follow with a survey** (validating) to quantify findings, measure preference, and provide the statistical backing that business cases require.

This two-phase approach costs more than either method alone, but it protects you from the two most common research failures:

- **Survey-only failure:** You get precise answers to the wrong questions because you didn't explore the space first.
- **Focus-group-only failure:** You have rich qualitative insights but can't convince stakeholders because "8 people in a room" doesn't satisfy their need for representative data.

## Singapore-Specific Considerations

### Language matters more than you think
In a survey, a respondent who is more comfortable in Mandarin but forced to answer in English will give you shorter, less nuanced, and potentially inaccurate responses. Always offer surveys in multiple languages. For focus groups, bilingual moderation is not a nice-to-have — it is a requirement for any project targeting the general Singapore population.

### Recruitment quality determines everything
Singapore is a small market. Panel fatigue is real. Professional survey-takers exist. The quality of your participant recruitment directly determines the quality of your data.

At Assembled, we recruit from our 100,000-member proprietary panel — built one person at a time, not purchased from third-party vendors. Every participant is verified, and we actively screen out professional respondents.

### Don't underestimate the "aunty and uncle" segment
Consumers aged 50+ control significant spending power in Singapore but are systematically underrepresented in online research. If your product or service targets this demographic, include offline recruitment methods and in-person or telephone interviews alongside online surveys.

### Sensitive topics require adjusted methods
For categories like health, finance, insurance, and personal care, social desirability bias is amplified in Singapore. In-depth interviews (one-on-one) consistently outperform group settings for these topics. Self-administered online surveys can also reduce bias for sensitive questions, provided the respondent trusts the anonymity.

## The Bottom Line

The right methodology is the one that answers your actual business question with the depth and confidence your decision requires. In Singapore's complex, multilingual, high-context market, that usually means starting qualitative and building to quantitative.

The most expensive research is research that gives you a false sense of certainty.
